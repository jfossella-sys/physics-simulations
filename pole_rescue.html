🧠 Before you play: Newton's First Law

Inertia means motion does not vanish when you stop pushing. An object changes its velocity only when a net external force acts on it. A rotating pole also has rotational inertia: changing its angular velocity requires a net torque.

① Position ≠ motion
A pole can pass through perfectly upright while still rotating. Watch the angular velocity (ω), not just its angle (θ).
② Slippery floor
With little friction, the pole's center of mass can keep moving sideways after you release a push. The bottom can move differently because the pole rotates.
③ Correct the motion
A push changes translation and rotation. Try small, timed pushes and watch the live force arrows: gravity ↓, normal ↑, friction ↔, push ↔.

🏆 Your mission

Bring the pole within ±3° of vertical, slow its rotation below 0.10 rad/s, and stop the base sliding faster than 0.10 m/s. Hold all three for 3 seconds.

The pole is a 2 m uniform rod with mass 2 kg. A fixed, horizontal force is applied at the selected height. The floor supports the lower tip until the rod falls.

How the physics works

The model integrates the rigid rod's translation and rotation together. It solves for the horizontal acceleration, angular acceleration, contact normal, and static or kinetic friction at every time step. Friction opposes sliding at the bottom, not necessarily the motion of the top.

Gravity acts at the center of mass. With no applied force, a vertical rod is an unstable equilibrium: even a small tilt produces gravitational torque. A successful rescue needs both angle and angular speed close to zero.

Teaching approximation: the lower tip stays in unilateral floor contact while the computed normal is positive. If contact is lost, the trial ends; a fully airborne rod is outside this version's model. The pole also stops at a 75° tilt or if it moves beyond the play area.
